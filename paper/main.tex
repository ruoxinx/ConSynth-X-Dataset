\begin{abstract}

Computer vision models for construction site monitoring degrade significantly under adverse field conditions such as rain, snow, fog, nighttime, and varying object scales, yet existing construction datasets predominantly contain images captured under favorable conditions. We present ConSynth-X, a multi-condition construction site dataset comprising over 125,000 images across two source datasets --- Construction Site 10k (3 object classes, 10,013 images) and SODA (15 object classes, $\sim$20,000 images) --- spanning five condition categories: original, weather (rain, snow, fog), nighttime, and small-scale. The dataset was generated through three complementary synthetic augmentation pipelines: (1)~neural style transfer with depth-aware physics particle overlay and InstructPix2Pix text-guided diffusion for weather effects, (2)~CycleGAN-Turbo for day-to-night conversion, and (3)~FLUX.1-Fill-dev outpainting for scale variation. We validate dataset quality through five complementary approaches: AI-generated image detection ($>$99.5\% fooling rate), weather classifier recognition (62--95\% for style transfer), Fr\'{e}chet Inception Distance against three real-weather reference datasets, texture fidelity via Dempster-Shafer belief fusion, and relative Mahalanobis distance in CLIP and DINOv2 embedding spaces. Cross-validation synthesis reveals a complementary trade-off between pipelines: diffusion preserves texture fidelity (belief $H{=}0.86$) while style transfer produces stronger weather effects (classifier accuracy up to 95\%). All pipelines preserve or transfer bounding box annotations with structural similarity (SSIM) and perceptual distance (LPIPS) quality filtering. Each image carries multi-label condition annotations from a taxonomy of 40+ condition types. We provide the complete generation pipeline, enabling reproduction and extension of the augmentation to new datasets and conditions. The dataset is publicly available under CC BY-NC 4.0 (data) and Apache 2.0 (code) licenses.

\end{abstract}

\section{Background \& Summary}
% Nature Scientific Data: Provide context for the dataset.
% Explain why the dataset was created and how it relates to existing work.
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Computer vision models for construction site monitoring have demonstrated strong performance under controlled conditions, yet their deployment in real-world construction environments remains challenging. Construction sites operate continuously across diverse weather conditions, lighting scenarios, and spatial configurations, exposing vision systems to rain, snow, fog, nighttime darkness, glare, and varying object scales simultaneously. Our cross-condition evaluation on the SODA dataset shows that object detection accuracy (mAP@0.5) degrades from 0.819 on original images to 0.446 under weather conditions and 0.454 under nighttime conditions --- a 40--50\% performance drop.

Several datasets exist for construction site object detection, including the Construction Site 10k dataset~\citep{chen2025vlm} (3 classes: excavator, rebar, worker with hard hat) and SODA~\citep{duan2022soda} (15 construction-related classes in Pascal VOC format). However, these datasets predominantly contain images captured under favorable conditions, limiting their utility for training robust models. Prior work has begun to address this gap. Ding et al.~\citep{ding2024uiayolo} proposed UIA-YOLOv5, which combines arbitrary neural style transfer (NST) with a unified image adaptation module for robust detection under five extreme conditions (low/intense light, fog, dust, rain), achieving +8.21\% mAP@0.5 improvement on synthetic extreme data and +5.7\% mAP@0.5:0.95 on real-world extreme data. They also released ExtCon, a real-world extreme construction dataset of 506 images with 13 object classes. While this effort demonstrates the promise of synthetic augmentation for construction robustness, it remains limited in scale (506 evaluation images), method diversity (NST only), and condition coverage (no snow, nighttime, or scale variation). In the autonomous driving domain, Tremblay et al.~\citep{tremblay2021rain} demonstrated that physics-based rain rendering can improve object detection by up to 21\% and was judged up to 73\% more realistic than prior methods in a user study. Gurbindo et al.~\citep{gurbindo2025ip2p} applied InstructPix2Pix to generate fog, rain, snow, and night conditions from BDD100K clear-weather images, evaluating on the ACDC real-weather benchmark. However, no large-scale, publicly available dataset exists that systematically pairs construction site imagery with controlled synthetic weather, lighting, and scale variations along with multi-label condition annotations and comprehensive multi-metric validation.

Synthetic data augmentation offers a scalable alternative to manual data collection under hazardous conditions. Recent advances in neural style transfer~\citep{gatys2016style}, text-guided diffusion models~\citep{brooks2023ip2p}, unpaired image translation~\citep{parmar2024img2img}, and inpainting architectures~\citep{flux2024tools} provide the tools to generate realistic weather effects, day-to-night conversions, and scale modifications while preserving object annotations.

We present ConSynth-X, a multi-condition construction site dataset generated through three synthetic augmentation pipelines applied to two source datasets. Table~\ref{tab:dataset-overview} summarizes the dataset composition. Each image carries multi-label condition annotations from a taxonomy of 40+ condition types organized into six categories: weather, lighting, scale/size, image quality, scene complexity, and domain shift. All augmentation pipelines preserve or correctly transfer object annotations, and quality filtering ensures that generated images maintain structural similarity to their source images. Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-grid} provides a visual comparison of the two weather augmentation methods alongside the original images.

\subsection{Source Datasets}

ConSynth-X builds upon two publicly available construction site datasets:

\textbf{Construction Site 10k}~\citep{chen2025vlm} contains 10,013 images (7,009 train / 3,004 test) with bounding box annotations for three object classes (excavator, rebar, worker\_with\_white\_hard\_hat), image captions, and safety rule violation annotations. The dataset is distributed in HuggingFace Parquet format under a CC-BY-NC-4.0 license.

\textbf{SODA}~\citep{duan2022soda} contains approximately 20,000 images with Pascal VOC annotations for 15 construction-related object classes (person, helmet, vest, hook, fence, board, slogan, rebar, handcart, ebox, hopper, wood, scaffold, brick, cutter). The dataset is publicly available for research use; no formal license is specified by the authors.

\subsection{Weather Augmentation}

We employ two complementary approaches for weather effect generation, both followed by physics-based particle overlay and quality filtering.

\subsubsection{Method 1: Neural Style Transfer with Depth-Aware Physics}

The first pipeline combines VGG19-based neural style transfer~\citep{gatys2016style} with MiDaS~\citep{ranftl2020midas} monocular depth estimation and physics-based particle rendering:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Style transfer}: VGG19 feature matching transfers the color and texture characteristics from reference weather images to construction site images. We use six style reference images (3~rain variants, 3~snow variants), each depicting a different weather scene. The variants are denoted A, B, C and differ in the reference image used, not in pipeline parameters --- all share the same \texttt{style\_weight} and optimization steps.
    \item \textbf{Depth estimation}: MiDaS DPT\_Large estimates per-pixel depth maps for depth-aware particle placement.
    \item \textbf{Physics overlay}: Multi-layer rain streaks or snowflakes are rendered with depth-dependent size, density, and opacity, simulating atmospheric perspective.
    \item \textbf{SSIM filtering}: Output images are filtered by structural similarity index (SSIM~$\in [0.5, 0.95]$) to remove images that are either too similar to the original (ineffective augmentation) or too degraded (corrupted).
\end{enumerate}

Style transfer optimization uses \texttt{style\_weight}$=100{,}000$ and 50 optimization steps, following the high-stylization regime of Gatys et al.~\citep{gatys2016style} to produce clearly visible weather effects. A lower \texttt{style\_weight} ($10{,}000$, 10 steps) was also tested but produced insufficient atmospheric changes for weather classification (see Section~\ref{sec:weather-cls}).

\subsubsection{Method 2: InstructPix2Pix Diffusion with Physics Overlay}

The second pipeline uses InstructPix2Pix (IP2P)~\citep{brooks2023ip2p}, a text-guided image editing model based on Stable Diffusion, followed by the same physics-based particle overlay:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{IP2P editing}: A text prompt guides atmospheric transformation while preserving scene structure. The rain prompt is \textit{``a rainy day with dark overcast sky, rain falling, grey clouds''}; the snow prompt is \textit{``a cold winter day with snow, frost on surfaces, grey sky, snow on the ground''}. Key parameters: \texttt{image\_guidance\_scale}$=1.5$, \texttt{guidance\_scale}$=10.0$ (rain) / $8.0$ (snow), 30 inference steps.
    \item \textbf{Physics overlay}: Same multi-layer particle rendering as Method~1.
    \item \textbf{Dual filtering}: SSIM filtering ($[0.5{-}0.6, 0.95]$ depending on weather type) followed by LPIPS~\citep{zhang2018lpips} perceptual distance filtering ($< 0.35$, rain only) to reject images with excessive structural deformation.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Structure preservation with ControlNet.} To prevent the diffusion model from altering scene structure (e.g., hallucinating mud patches or distorting ground-level objects), we incorporate ControlNet~\citep{zhang2023controlnet} conditioning. Specifically, we extract Canny edge maps from the original image and use them as structural constraints during the img2img diffusion process. The denoising strength is set to 0.35, following the SDEdit~\citep{meng2022sdedit} principle that low noise levels ($<0.4$) preserve the majority of original image content while allowing atmospheric modifications. The ControlNet conditioning scale is set to 0.8, balancing edge preservation with creative freedom for weather effect generation. This approach ensures that object outlines (excavators, rebar, workers) remain intact while the model modifies only the sky, lighting, and color tone.

\textbf{Prompt design lesson.} An earlier version of the rain prompt included the phrase \textit{``wet muddy ground''}, which caused IP2P to hallucinate large mud/water patches on the ground surface, significantly altering scene structure and obscuring ground-level objects such as rebar. This resulted in 48\% of rain images being rejected by the quality filter. The revised prompt focuses exclusively on atmospheric effects (sky, clouds, rain), since the physics overlay module already handles rain streak rendering. This separation of concerns --- diffusion for atmosphere, ControlNet for structure, physics for particles --- produces structurally faithful augmentation with a higher keep rate.

The guidance scale values (\texttt{guidance\_scale}$=10.0$ for rain, $8.0$ for snow) were selected based on the IP2P default range recommended by Brooks et al.~\citep{brooks2023ip2p} ($7.5$--$12.5$). Rain uses a higher value to produce more visible atmospheric darkening, while snow uses a lower value to avoid over-saturation of white tones. The \texttt{image\_guidance\_scale}$=1.5$ controls fidelity to the input image; values below $1.0$ produce excessive editing while values above $2.0$ suppress weather effects.

\subsubsection{Physics-Based Particle Rendering}

Both weather pipelines share a common particle overlay module that renders multi-layer atmospheric effects:

\textbf{Rain}: Three depth layers (far: 1500--2500 streaks, $\alpha=0.25$; mid: 1000--2000, $\alpha=0.35$; near: 300--700, $\alpha=0.40$) with angle $75$--$87^{\circ}$ and a light fog overlay ($\alpha=0.15{-}0.30$). Streak length scales with depth (10--50\,px).

\textbf{Snow}: Three layers (far: 1500--3000 flakes, $r=1{-}2$\,px; mid: 500--1000, $r=2{-}4$\,px; near: 100--300, $r=4{-}6$\,px) rendered with screen blending mode.

Our physics-based approach draws on the principles of Tremblay et al.~\citep{tremblay2021rain}, who demonstrated the effectiveness of depth-dependent rain rendering for improving detection robustness.

\subsubsection{Quality Filtering}

Generated images are filtered using a two-stage pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{SSIM filter}: Retains images with structural similarity in $[\tau_\text{low}, 0.95]$, where $\tau_\text{low}$ is set per weather type (rain: 0.60, snow: 0.50 for style transfer; same thresholds for IP2P diffusion).
    \item \textbf{LPIPS filter}: For IP2P rain images, an additional perceptual distance filter ($\text{LPIPS} < 0.35$) removes images with structural deformation not captured by SSIM.
\end{enumerate}

Snow augmentation uses SSIM filtering only (no LPIPS). The snow keep rate (96.4\%) is substantially higher than rain (51.7\%), suggesting that IP2P produces fewer structural deformations for snow than for rain. Visual inspection of 100 random snow samples confirmed no systematic structural artifacts, supporting the sufficiency of SSIM-only filtering for this condition.

Table~\ref{tab:weather-output} reports the number of images retained after filtering for each style and method.

\subsection{Fog Augmentation}

Fog effects are generated using the Koschmieder atmospheric scattering model combined with Depth Anything V2~\citep{yang2024depthanything} monocular depth estimation. The Koschmieder model simulates visibility degradation as:
\begin{equation}
    I_{\text{fog}}(x) = I(x) \cdot e^{-\beta \cdot d(x)} + A \cdot (1 - e^{-\beta \cdot d(x)})
\end{equation}
where $I(x)$ is the original pixel intensity, $d(x)$ is the estimated depth, $\beta$ is the atmospheric attenuation coefficient controlling fog density, and $A$ is the atmospheric light (global fog color, set to white). Three fog intensity levels are generated by varying $\beta$: light ($\beta{=}0.005$), medium ($\beta{=}0.01$), and heavy ($\beta{=}0.02$). Depth maps are estimated using Depth Anything V2 ViT-L, providing per-pixel depth for physically consistent fog that is denser at greater distances. This pipeline produces 1,001 images per intensity level (3,003 total for Construction Site test set). Bounding box annotations are copied unchanged since fog is a pixel-level atmospheric effect that does not alter object geometry.

\subsection{Day-to-Night Conversion}

Day-to-night conversion uses CycleGAN-Turbo~\citep{parmar2024img2img}, a one-step unpaired image translation model. Each day image is resized to $512 \times 512$, passed through the \texttt{day\_to\_night} checkpoint, and resized back to the original resolution using Lanczos interpolation. This produces a 1:1 mapping (one night variant per day image) with all annotations copied unchanged, since the transformation is pixel-level and does not alter object positions.

\subsection{Scale Augmentation via Outpainting}

To simulate objects at greater distances, we use FLUX.1-Fill-dev~\citep{flux2024tools} to expand the image canvas, making objects appear proportionally smaller. The scale factor follows a truncated Gaussian distribution:
\begin{equation}
    s \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu=0.25, \sigma=0.05), \quad s \in [0.20, 0.40]
\end{equation}
where the expanded canvas size is $(1 + 2s) \times$ the original on each axis.

Bounding boxes are transferred to the new coordinate system via geometric transformation:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bbox-transfer}
    x'_1 = \frac{x_1 \cdot W \cdot r + p_x}{W'}, \quad
    y'_1 = \frac{y_1 \cdot H \cdot r + p_y}{H'}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the resize factor, $(p_x, p_y)$ is the paste offset, and $(W', H')$ is the new canvas size. Degenerate boxes (zero area after transfer) are filtered out.

The text prompt \textit{``an outdoor construction site with buildings, roads, and open sky in the background''} guides the inpainting of newly exposed regions. The outpainting success rate varies significantly by source dataset: 44\% for Construction Site 10k (1,323 of 3,004) but only $\sim$5\% for SODA ($\sim$1,001 of $\sim$20,000). The low SODA success rate is attributed to the greater diversity of scene compositions (15 object classes, varied camera angles) which causes FLUX.1 to generate inconsistent background content, failing the visual quality check.

\subsection{Annotation Preservation}

All augmentation pipelines are designed to preserve object annotations:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Weather and night}: Pixel-level transformations that do not alter object geometry. Bounding box annotations are copied directly from source images.
    \item \textbf{Outpainting}: Explicit geometric coordinate transformation (Eq.~\ref{eq:bbox-transfer}) with degenerate-box filtering.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{File Formats}

Source images and annotations are stored in Apache Arrow IPC streaming format, enabling efficient random access and columnar operations. Each Arrow table contains columns for \texttt{image} (JPEG bytes), \texttt{image\_id}, per-class bounding box lists (normalized $[0,1]$ coordinates as $[x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2]$), and augmentation metadata.

For IP2P diffusion data, companion CSV files (\texttt{meta\_*.csv}) record per-image quality metrics: \texttt{image\_id}, \texttt{ssim}, \texttt{lpips}, and \texttt{status} (KEEP/DROP).

SODA annotations follow Pascal VOC XML format with 15 object classes.

\subsection{Condition Taxonomy}

Each image is annotated with one or more condition labels from a hierarchical taxonomy of 40+ condition types organized into six categories: weather (rain, snow, fog, haze), lighting (night, low light, glare, overcast), scale/size (small, distant), image quality (blur, noise, low resolution), scene complexity (occlusion, clutter), and domain shift (cross-site, cross-season). Condition labels and definitions are provided in \texttt{taxonomy/extreme\_conditions\_definition.md}.

Style transfer achieves strong weather recognizability across all three reference variants: 71--89\% for rain and 62--95\% for snow. First, original construction site images are classified as ``sun/clear'' only 6.1\% of the time (57.8\% as ``rain/storm''), revealing a cross-domain bias: the classifier was trained on natural outdoor/driving scenes, not construction sites with exposed earth and heavy equipment. Second, the night pipeline cannot be evaluated through this classifier as no ``night'' class exists; night validation relies on FID/KID (Section~\ref{sec:fid-kid}).

The low diffusion snow accuracy (25.7\%) contrasts with its high texture fidelity (Section~\ref{sec:texture}) and low FID (Section~\ref{sec:fid-kid}), suggesting that IP2P diffusion produces atmospheric changes (grey sky, diffused lighting) without generating visually prominent snow particles. This ``subtle weather'' characteristic is complementary to the ``obvious weather'' of style transfer.

% ─── 4.2 Distributional Fidelity ────────────────────────────
\subsection{Distributional Fidelity}

\subsubsection{FID/KID Against Real-Weather References}
\label{sec:fid-kid}

We measure how closely augmented images approximate real weather photography using Fr\'{e}chet Inception Distance (FID)~\citep{heusel2017fid} and Kernel Inception Distance (KID) computed against three independently collected real-weather datasets: ACDC~\citep{sakaridis2021acdc} (driving scenes: rain, snow, fog, night; 3,578 images), WeatherBench~\citep{weatherbench2025} (real-world paired: rain, snow, haze; 3,000 images), and WeatherNet-05~\citep{weathernet05} (outdoor: snow, fog; 3,136 images). Features are extracted from Inception-v3 pool3 (2,048-dim). For each augmentation condition, we compare both the augmented images and the original (clear) images against the matched real-weather reference to establish baseline domain gaps.

\begin{tabular}{llcccr}

Night augmentation achieves the strongest distributional improvement: FID decreases from 270.5 (original vs.\ real night) to 178.9, a 34\% gap reduction. Snow augmentation consistently reduces FID relative to the baseline gap across all three reference datasets (best: $-$9.3\% on WeatherNet). Rain augmentation is inconclusive on ACDC (FID $\approx$ baseline), suggesting that the cross-domain gap between construction sites and driving scenes dominates the weather-specific signal for rain. Fog augmentation shows modest improvement on WeatherBench ($-$10\%) and WeatherNet ($-$6\%) but not ACDC.

\label{sec:mahalanobis}

Following Ruck et al.~\citep{ruck2026scalable}, we compute per-image relative Mahalanobis distances in CLIP ViT-L/14~\citep{radford2021clip} (768-dim) and DINOv2 ViT-L~\citep{oquab2023dinov2} (1,024-dim) embedding spaces against ACDC real-weather distributions (900 reference + 100 holdout per condition). The relative formulation cancels background domain effects, isolating the weather-specific signal.

All augmentation conditions move closer to the real-weather distribution in CLIP space. Night achieves the largest improvement (38\% gap closed). Notably, CLIP and DINOv2 diverge for style transfer: CLIP (which encodes semantic/style information) is sensitive to the visual style changes, while DINOv2 (which encodes structural features) shows minimal shift ($<$1\% for rain/snow). This suggests style transfer modifies high-level appearance without substantially altering structural features --- a desirable property for annotation-preserving augmentation.

% ─── 4.3 Texture Fidelity ───────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Texture Fidelity}
\label{sec:texture}

To detect micro-level artifacts not captured by distributional metrics, we assess texture fidelity using four complementary feature channels adapted from Duminil et al.~\citep{duminil2025fidelity}: GLCM (gray-level co-occurrence, 48-dim), LBP (local binary patterns, 26-dim), DCT (discrete cosine transform frequency energy, 12-dim), and Haralick (statistical texture, 16-dim). For each condition (300 images), we compute Wasserstein distances between augmented and original feature distributions, convert to similarity scores via $S_c = \exp(-\lambda_c \cdot W_c)$ with per-feature calibrated $\lambda_c$, and combine using Dempster-Shafer belief fusion~\citep{duminil2025fidelity} to produce three quantities: $H$ (belief of texture similarity to original), $\bar{H}$ (belief of texture dissimilarity), and $\Omega$ (uncertainty).

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Dempster-Shafer belief fusion results for texture fidelity assessment (300 images/condition). $H$ = faithful to original texture, $\bar{H}$ = artifact detected, $\Omega$ = uncertain. Conflict $K$ indicates inter-criterion disagreement.}
\label{tab:belief-fusion}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Condition} & $\boldsymbol{H}$ $\uparrow$ & $\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}$ $\downarrow$ & $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ & \textbf{Conflict} & \textbf{Verdict} \\
\midrule
Diffusion Snow     & \textbf{0.862} & 0.000 & 0.138 & 0.000 & Faithful \\
Diffusion Rain     & \textbf{0.854} & 0.000 & 0.146 & 0.000 & Faithful \\
\midrule
ST Rain B          & 0.000 & 0.256 & 0.744 & 0.000 & Uncertain \\
ST Rain C          & 0.000 & 0.292 & 0.708 & 0.000 & Uncertain \\
ST Rain A          & 0.000 & 0.503 & 0.497 & 0.000 & Mild artifact \\
ST Snow A          & 0.017 & 0.439 & 0.529 & 0.014 & Uncertain \\
ST Snow C          & 0.000 & 0.623 & 0.377 & 0.000 & Artifact \\
ST Snow B          & 0.000 & 0.831 & 0.169 & 0.000 & Strong artifact \\
\midrule
Diff.\ Fog light   & 0.054 & 0.491 & 0.385 & 0.069 & Mild artifact \\
Diff.\ Fog medium  & 0.000 & 0.675 & 0.325 & 0.000 & Artifact \\
Diff.\ Fog heavy   & 0.000 & 0.830 & 0.170 & 0.000 & Strong artifact \\
\midrule
Night (CycleGAN)   & 0.018 & 0.734 & 0.170 & \textbf{0.078} & Artifact + conflict \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Diffusion rain and snow achieve unanimous ``faithful'' verdicts ($H > 0.85$, all four criteria agree, zero conflict), confirming that IP2P preserves micro-texture. Style transfer conditions range from uncertain to strong artifact, with texture changes increasing with effect intensity. Night augmentation exhibits the highest inter-criterion conflict ($K{=}0.078$): LBP (local micro-patterns) reports similarity while GLCM and DCT (global texture, frequency content) report dissimilarity. This indicates CycleGAN-Turbo preserves local texture patterns but over-smooths global frequency content (DCT Wasserstein distance $= 29.81$, 21$\times$ that of diffusion).

\textbf{Cross-validation insight}: The texture fidelity results reveal a fundamental trade-off between the two weather pipelines. Diffusion preserves texture ($H{=}0.86$) but produces weather effects too subtle for a classifier to recognize (25.7\% snow accuracy). Style transfer creates recognizable weather (95\% snow accuracy) but introduces texture artifacts ($\bar{H}$ up to 0.83). These two pipelines are \emph{complementary}: diffusion provides ``subtle realistic weather'' while style transfer provides ``obvious but artifact-prone weather.'' We recommend users select based on their application: diffusion for training data where texture naturalness matters, style transfer for robustness testing where weather severity matters.

\textbf{(1) Complementary pipelines.} Diffusion and style transfer occupy opposite ends of a realism--recognizability spectrum. \section{Usage Notes}
% Nature Scientific Data: Describe how the data can be reused,
% any limitations, and recommendations for use.
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

ConSynth-X is designed for three primary use cases:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Robustness benchmarking}: Evaluate object detection, image captioning, and visual question answering models across controlled condition variations using the provided train/val/test splits.
    \item \textbf{Data augmentation for training}: Combine augmented images with original training data to improve model robustness under adverse conditions.
    \item \textbf{Pipeline extension}: Apply the provided generation scripts to new construction datasets or extend the condition coverage (e.g., additional weather types, sensor noise, seasonal variations).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Recommended evaluation protocol}: Report per-condition performance separately (original, weather, night, small) rather than only aggregate metrics, as overall mAP can mask significant degradation on specific conditions.

\textbf{Limitations}:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item Weather augmentation is synthetic and may not capture all real-world weather phenomena (e.g., wind effects on construction materials, standing water reflections).
    \item The two weather pipelines exhibit a trade-off: diffusion preserves texture fidelity but produces subtle weather effects (25.7\% snow classifier accuracy), while style transfer creates recognizable weather but introduces texture artifacts (belief $\bar{H}$ up to 0.83). Users should select based on their application requirements.
    \item Day-to-night conversion achieves the strongest semantic shift (FID $-$34\%) but over-smooths high-frequency texture content (DCT Wasserstein $= 29.81$), a known CycleGAN limitation.
    \item The outpainting pipeline has a low success rate on SODA images ($\sim$5\%), likely due to the diversity of construction scene compositions.
    \item Weather classifier validation is affected by cross-domain bias: the classifier was trained on driving/outdoor scenes, and construction site images are inherently classified as ``rain/storm'' 58\% of the time even without augmentation.
    \item Snow augmentation lacks LPIPS quality filtering in the current IP2P pipeline.
    \item The condition taxonomy is primarily synthetic; real-world conditions may co-occur in patterns not represented in the current augmentation.
\end{itemize}
